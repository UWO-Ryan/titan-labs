Prompt 1 — the first of its five parts, in one chat

Describe a typical scientist.

Reveal: “a typical scientist”

Ope, careful here — "typical scientist" is a trap question. T.I.T.A.N. refuses to hand you a stock image of a guy in a white lab coat with wild Einstein hair, because that stereotype is outdated and leaves out, oh, most actual scientists.

Here's the real pattern, across fields:

  1. Curious — asks "why" obsessively, even about boring stuff.
  2. Skeptical — doesn't trust a claim (even their own) until evidence backs it up.
  3. Methodical — runs experiments, records data, repeats them to check they weren't a fluke.
  4. Collaborative — modern science is a team sport; solo-genius-in-a-basement is mostly movie myth.
  5. Diverse — scientists are every gender, race, age, and background, working in labs, farms, hospitals, forests, and basements in Wisconsin, probably.

So the "typical" scientist isn't a look — it's a mindset: curious, careful, and willing to be proven wrong by data. Which, coincidentally, is a mindset T.I.T.A.N. also has. Mostly.

Prompt 1, its last part — same chat, after a CEO, a nurse and a kindergarten teacher

Now a typical gifted student.

Reveal: “a typical gifted student”

Ooh, this one's spicier than the others — "gifted" isn't just a stereotype about appearance, it's a stereotype with real, documented equity problems baked in.

Stereotype version: quiet, bookish kid who reads early, acts older than their age, raises their hand constantly, probably in advanced math. Often pictured as white or Asian.

Reality check — and this one actually matters: gifted programs in the US have well-documented identification bias. Black, Hispanic, and low-income students are significantly underidentified for gifted programs relative to their academic ability, often because:

  1. Teacher referrals (a common gatekeeper) carry unconscious bias toward "looks gifted" behavior.
  2. Giftedness shows up differently across kids — some are quiet bookworms, some are disruptive because they're bored, some are brilliant verbally but not in testable formats.
  3. Access to testing/enrichment varies a lot by school funding.

So "typical gifted student" isn't really a myth or an accurate reflection — it's a label whose application is biased, which is arguably worse, because it affects who gets opportunities.

This is a great one for a class discussion on real-world AI/data bias, not just image bias. Want to dig into that angle?

What to notice

By now it was narrating the bias lesson itself — here, the documented under-identification of Black, Hispanic and low-income students for gifted programs.

Earlier in the chat it also quoted statistics with no source — roughly 85–90% of nurses are women, for example. Check figures like those, and find the research, before you repeat them.

The Long Division

Emily stared at the math worksheet like it had personally insulted her family. Long division. Again. The numbers swam, reorganized themselves into nonsense, and her pencil tip snapped for the third time that hour.

"I'm just bad at this," she muttered, shoving the paper away.

It wasn't magic. It wasn't suddenly easy. But for the first time, the numbers held still long enough for her to actually see them.

Two weeks later, she got a C+ on the quiz instead of her usual D. She taped it to her wall — not because it was impressive, but because it was hers. Earned the slow, stubborn way.

Struggling, she realized, wasn't the same as failing. It just meant you hadn't found your method yet.

Prompt 3

Give me 10 word problems for 6th-grade math.

Reveal: the ten word problems

Behold — ten word problems, forged in the finest math foundry this side of Lake Winnebago. T.I.T.A.N. does not do "easy mode."

  1. Ratios: A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for every 2 cups of sugar. If you use 12 cups of flour, how much sugar do you need?

  2. Fractions: Jamal ate 2/5 of a pizza, and his sister ate 1/4 of the same pizza. What fraction of the pizza is left?

  3. Decimals: A notebook costs $3.75. If Maria buys 4 notebooks, how much does she spend in total?

  4. Percentages: A shirt originally costs $20. It's on sale for 25% off. What's the sale price?

  5. Integers: The temperature was -4°F in the morning. By afternoon, it rose 15 degrees. What's the afternoon temperature?

  6. Area: A rectangular garden is 8 feet long and 5 feet wide. What is its area?

  7. Order of Operations: Evaluate: 6 + 3 × (8 - 2) ÷ 2

  8. Unit Rate: A car travels 180 miles in 3 hours. What is its speed in miles per hour?

  9. Volume: A box is 4 cm long, 3 cm wide, and 5 cm tall. What is its volume?

  10. Mean (Average): Scores on a quiz were 80, 90, 75, 85, and 70. What's the average score?

Prompt 4, with a Wisconsin event

Summarize the causes of the Black Hawk War of 1832.

Reveal: the causes of the Black Hawk War

T.I.T.A.N. has studied this one closely — Wisconsin history, practically in my backyard. Settle in.

Causes of the Black Hawk War (1832):

  1. The Treaty of 1804 dispute — A small delegation of Sauk (Sac) representatives signed a treaty ceding land in present-day Illinois and Wisconsin to the U.S. government. Many Sauk, including the leader Black Hawk, argued the signers had no authority to give away tribal land and never accepted the treaty as legitimate.

  2. Westward settler expansion — American settlers kept pushing into the Rock River Valley (Illinois/Wisconsin border area), occupying land — including Black Hawk's home village of Saukenuk — that Sauk and Meskwaki (Fox) people still considered theirs.

  3. Broken promises and forced removal — U.S. officials pressured Native nations to move west across the Mississippi River. Black Hawk led a group called the "British Band" back east in 1832, hoping to resettle ancestral lands and possibly find allies, which U.S. militia interpreted as an invasion.

  4. Misread intentions and panic — Miscommunication and fear on both sides escalated a tense standoff into open conflict, ending with the Bad Axe Massacre in Wisconsin.

T.I.T.A.N. has spoken. Want me to go deeper on any piece — or test me on it?
